## 1. Headline Conclusions

The most important analytical result of this review is a convergence reached by two independent agents via structurally different routes: the distributed-generation pathway that hyperscalers are rapidly building is not an alternative to fossil entrenchment — it is a parallel mechanism of fossil entrenchment. The energy-market analyst established this via OEM supply economics (combined GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Power backlog of approximately 190 GW, representing roughly five years of current manufacturing output, physically caps central CCGT buildout from the PJM TC1 queue). The reference-class analyst established this independently via historical base-rate analysis (US combined-heat-and-power capacity has plateaued near 7% of national capacity since 1990; microgrids represent 0.4% of US installed capacity as of 2023) and project-mix decomposition (approximately 95% of announced 2024–2026 hyperscaler on-site capacity is gas-fired). Both paths arrive at the same conclusion: the mechanism of bridge-fuel entrenchment shifts from utility-scale CCGT central plant to distributed reciprocating engine (RICE) fleets and intensified use of the existing combined-cycle fleet. This reframes, but does not weaken, the prior review's bridge-fuel-entrenchment finding. Behind-the-meter gas has no public utility commission retirement pathway — it may be structurally harder to unwind than central CCGT, compounding stranded-asset exposure beyond what the prior review identified via thermal-efficiency arguments alone.

A second major result is a deliberate divergence that must not be collapsed to consensus: the tech-industry analyst and the reference-class analyst disagree on the probability of continued AI demand growth. The tech-industry analyst places continued high-growth (IEA Base Case, approximately 1,200 TWh globally by 2035) at roughly 40–50% probability, with plateau at 25–35% and downside as non-negligible. The reference-class analyst, applying historical sectoral-surge base rates (surges above 25% CAGR almost never persist more than 5–8 years), places plateau at 30–45% probability, matching or exceeding the continued-growth case at 25–40%, with downside at 15–25%. Both draw on T1-tier sources; the difference is interpretive, not evidentiary. Procurement decisions embedded in current nuclear PPAs and the PJM gas queue implicitly assume continued high growth. Both agents agree this framing carries significant demand-mismatch risk, but they weight that risk differently. This synthesis preserves both positions.

A third headline: the medium-term clean-firm scaling picture is more constrained than prior-review framing implied, and the constraints are specific. SMR commercial fleet by 2035 reaches 5 GW only under aggressive-optimistic conditions (approximately 20–30% probability). LDES at hyperscaler scale is gated by Form Energy's unverified $20/kWh cost target. Eavor closed-loop geothermal proves concept at small scale but is a 2035–2040 contributor at earliest, not a 2030–2035 lever. Post-Hub green hydrogen probability per hub falls to 10–15%, revised down from the prior review's 15–30%. Taken together, the conjunctive probability that all three of the H1 conditions are met by 2035 — 5+ GW SMR, LDES coordination, and 25+ GW interregional transmission — is low, probably below 15% in the assessment across the agent team.

Long-term clean-firm prospects are real but require disambiguation. Fusion before 2040 is a tail probability; before 2050 it is a marginal contributor at central estimate. Advanced nuclear can follow positive learning trajectories, but only if site-level conditions (single design, single integrated operator, supply-chain depth) that are absent from the US default deployment structure are achieved — and the Korean APR-1400 analogy, frequently invoked in optimistic nuclear analysis, does not straightforwardly transfer to US SMR deployment.

## 2. Medium-Term Picture (2030–2040)

### SMR Fleet Realism

The agent review converges on a two-category disaggregation of clean nuclear addition by 2035. Category A (LWR restarts and uprates — Palisades approximately 800 MW targeting 2026, TMI/Crane approximately 835 MW, Duane Arnold 601 MW targeting 2029, uprate pipeline adding 2.5–5 GW through 2035) carries higher near-term probability and represents the primary near-term nuclear clean-firm contribution. Category B — genuine new-build SMR and advanced reactor construction — is the focus of most forward-looking analysis and is where divergence from prior review framing is sharpest.

The energy-market analyst assessed Category B at 0.5–2 GW base case, 2–4 GW realistic-optimistic, and 4–6 GW aggressive-optimistic by 2035, with the 5 GW H1 threshold achievable only at aggressive-optimistic (approximately 20–30% probability), citing project-level NRC filing status, 80% FOAK overrun base rate, and schedule dependency on Natrium, Xe-100 Dow, and at least one additional project all succeeding simultaneously [F[EMA-1]]. The reference-class analyst converged on a 2–4 GW central estimate with 80% CI of [1 GW, 5 GW], via reference-class and conditioning-factor analysis rather than project enumeration [F-RCA-F4]. The two agents arrived at compatible central cases via independent routes — this is genuine cross-agent convergence.

The economics analyst deepened this picture with a structural diagnosis of why the Korean APR-1400 analogy does not transfer to the US SMR default deployment structure. The Korean program achieved positive nth-of-a-kind learning via four conditions: design freeze across units, vertically integrated constructor-designer-operator, continuous domestic build cadence, and government-coordinated supply chain [F[EA-1]]. The economics analyst's initial framing — "Korean analogy misapplied" — was refined by the devil's advocate to a more precise claim: Korean site-level learning conditions are absent from the US default multi-utility multi-state deployment structure. An alternative learning mechanism operating at the factory or module level (rather than site level) is plausible and is what optimistic US SMR projections implicitly rely upon, but it is unproven and the burden of proof rests on demonstrating positive learning at any scale rather than refuting Korean transferability [DA synthesis mandate 1]. Vogtle-style negative-or-flat learning is the historical reference class for US/EU large-reactor programs since 1995, with approximately 70–85% of programs in that class showing negative outcomes.

The reference-class analyst separately flagged the HALEU supply ceiling as the binding constraint on the aggressive-optimistic Category B scenario that neither EMA nor RCA quantified directly. Centrus currently produces approximately 12 MT/year of HALEU at Piketon; combined demand for a 4–6 GW fleet across Natrium, Xe-100, Kairos KP-FHR, Oklo, and Holtec SMR-300 likely exceeds 30–50 MT/year. The 20–30% aggressive-scenario probability from the energy-market analyst should be treated as conditional on HALEU supply expanding, which is not a guaranteed outcome.

### Conventional Nuclear Restart Pipeline

The conventional nuclear restart and uprate pipeline contributes a realistic 2–4 GW of additional clean firm power by 2030, growing to 3–5 GW by 2035 as the uprate backlog clears NRC review [F[EMA-2]]. This is the highest-probability clean-nuclear pathway in the medium term. Key delivery risks: Palisades has already slipped from end-2025 to 2026+; Duane Arnold faces a Beyond Nuclear intervention petition; the NRC review queue creates simultaneous-processing pressure across 30+ uprate applications. A 12–18 month schedule risk buffer should be applied to all announced dates.

### Enhanced Geothermal Beyond Fervo

Eavor's closed-loop advanced baseload geothermal technology proved concept at Geretsried, Germany (first grid power December 2025, 4-loop system generating 8 MWe plus 64 MWth in combined-heat-and-power configuration). However, the 8 MWe per four-loop system reveals a significant scale challenge: reaching 100–500 MW of firm power for a data center campus requires 50–250 or more parallel systems and a coordinated drilling campaign that does not exist at that scale today. Eavor closed-loop is a 2035–2040 contributor for US AI data center supply under optimistic assumptions, not a 2030–2035 lever. Open-loop EGS in the Basin and Range (building on Fervo's Cape Phase I success, covered in the prior review) remains the nearer-term firm-power pathway for specific geographies [F[EMA-3]]. The DOE Enhanced Geothermal Earthshot target of $45/MWh by 2035 requires approximately 90% cost reduction from the 2022 baseline; DOE SunShot succeeded at a comparable task for solar PV, but no 2025–2026 on-track confirmation exists for the geothermal equivalent.

### Post-Hub Green Hydrogen 2030–2032

The conditional probability that green hydrogen primary power for data centers becomes competitive at hub geographies by 2030–2032 has been revised down to 10–15% per hub, from the prior review's 15–30%. The DOE Liftoff Report December 2024 update showed developer cost estimates rising to $5–7/kg — the wrong direction from the $1/kg Hydrogen Shot target. Four conjunctive dependencies must all hold: 45V credit political survival, large-scale electrolyzer cost realization (ScienceDirect 2025 EU dataset showed no clear cost reductions at large scale), hub buildout proceeding, and PEM 32.1% learning rate applying at utility scale [F[EMA-4]]. Gulf Coast HALO H2Hub remains the highest-probability geography. This is a monitored option, not a planning assumption.

### LDES at Scale and the Cross-Agent $20/kWh Gap

Three agents independently identified Form Energy's $20/kWh-stored target as unverified, arriving via different analytical routes: the energy-market analyst through Lazard LCOS comparison (current iron-air at $35–50/kWh plus RTE penalty yields blended LCOE of $90–115/MWh, not the sub-$80/MWh threshold implied by the $20/kWh target) [F[EMA-5]]; the tech-industry analyst through source-type audit (the $20/kWh figure is a company manufacturing roadmap statement, not independently audited) [F[TIA-4]]; and the economics analyst through Google Minnesota deal-implied pricing ($33/kWh post-45X credits, approximately $77/kWh pre-credit, with Form Factory 1 targeting 500 MW/year production) [F[EA-2]]. The economics analyst's realistic 2030 range is $30–50/kWh installed, which makes behind-the-meter economics viable post-45X credit but not at the optimistic blended LCOE projection. Georgia Power's 15 MW/1.5 GWh project (announced 2026, first independent cost data point expected 2027–2028) gates interpretation of the Google Minnesota economics. The Google Minnesota project itself gates the broader H9 hypothesis about 85–95% clean energy at hyperscaler scale.

Iron-air's round-trip efficiency (45–50%, versus lithium-ion at 85–90%) requires approximately 1.7–2× the solar PV capacity for equivalent net energy delivery, materially increasing land use and capital cost in any blended LCOE calculation.

The economics analyst's broader LDES picture is that $30–50/kWh is achievable in 2030; sub-$80/MWh blended LCOE has 30–40% probability by 2028 and 50–65% probability by 2032 if Form Factory 1 scales and learning activates. The coordination-failure gate (who builds first at the required scale?) is identified as the primary non-cost constraint — the technology has a viable path, but deployment requires an anchor commitment.

### Continental Transmission Backbone

The geopolitical strategist assessed realistic US interregional transmission capacity addition by 2035 at approximately 10–22 GW, materially below the NERC ITCS-identified 35 GW minimum need [F[GS-1]]. The binding constraints are structural and co-equal: state-federal jurisdiction fragmentation, cost allocation disputes across RTO seams, eminent domain variability by state, and NIMBY opposition. Historical pace since 2014 is approximately 0.6 GW/year of large-scale interregional transmission, versus the 3.9 GW/year required to meet the NERC 35 GW target in nine years.

Key projects: SunZia HVDC approximately 3 GW, Champlain Hudson Power Express approximately 1.25 GW, Grain Belt Express Phase 1 approximately 5 GW (DOE $4.9B loan terminated July 2025, pursuing private financing), TransWest Express approximately 3 GW, SOO Green approximately 2.1 GW. Optimistic total 14–15 GW, realistic 10–12 GW. MISO Tranche 2.1 ($21.8B, 3,631 miles, approved December 2024, in-service 2032–2034) enables significant intra-MISO renewable capacity but serves intra-MISO not interregional RTO-to-RTO transfer and does not count toward the NERC 35 GW interregional gap. An important misattribution to correct in existing analysis: MISO T2.1 is sometimes cited in discussion of interregional capacity expansion, but it addresses a different metric.

Reconductoring of existing lines is a genuine wildcard that could add 10–20 GW of effective interregional capacity without new right-of-way disputes, potentially raising the upper bound to 22–30 GW under optimistic assumptions. This does not change the core conclusion that the NERC 35 GW need will not be met by 2035.

### AI Demand Trajectory Beyond 2030 (Deliberate Divergence — Preserve Both Positions)

The tech-industry analyst and the reference-class analyst disagree on AI demand trajectory and this synthesis does not force consensus.

The tech-industry analyst's position: continued high-growth (IEA Base Case, approximately 1,200 TWh globally by 2035) is approximately the most likely outcome at 40–50% probability; plateau (IEA Headwinds, approximately 700 TWh by 2035) is a credible minority scenario at 25–35%. Supporting evidence includes Jevons paradox (per-token inference costs dropped approximately 100× from 2022–2025 while usage increased approximately 1,000×), $700B in committed 2026 hyperscaler capex locking in demand infrastructure for 5–7 years, and Epoch AI analysis projecting frontier training runs requiring 4–16 GW individually by 2030 [F[TIA-1]].

The reference-class analyst's position: plateau-after-2032 has approximately 30–45% probability, tying or slightly exceeding continued 30% CAGR through 2035 at 25–40%. Historical sectoral surges (railway electrification, refrigeration, air-conditioning, internet) almost never sustain above 25% CAGR for more than 10 years; AI appears different in Jevons elasticity but this may not override the structural deceleration that arrives as each sectoral surge matures [F-RCA-F2].

Observable signals that update toward each scenario: continued growth is supported by sustained capex growth above 30% year-over-year in 2027–2028 and GPU utilization above 80%; plateau is supported by capex guidance cuts above 20% in any single quarter and sustained GPU utilization slipping below 60%. The procurement implications differ: the tech-industry analyst's position means current nuclear PPAs and gas queue commitments are rational volume bets; the reference-class analyst's position means these commitments are over-buying with 55–70% probability, though the asymmetric downside (AI workload rationing) drives the observed over-commitment regardless of which scenario obtains.

## 3. Long-Term Picture (2040+)

### Fusion Realism

The economics analyst assessed fusion's contribution to AI power at 0–2 GW by 2040 conditional on Commonwealth Fusion Systems ARC reaching commercial replication, with Helion's 2028 commitment to Microsoft carrying 15–25% probability of on-time delivery [F[EA-3]]. The reference-class analyst, after XVERIFY challenge that widened the confidence interval, places AI-allocated fusion at [0 GW, 12 GW] by 2040 with a central estimate of 1–3 GW, and [0 GW, 60 GW] by 2050 with a central estimate of 10–25 GW [F-RCA-F3].

The practical planning conclusion is shared across both agents: fusion is a tail-probability contributor for AI power by 2040 and should not appear as a planning assumption in infrastructure decisions for the 2025–2040 window. By 2050, it is a potential marginal contributor. CFS ARC (SPARC first plasma 2026, ARC targeting early 2030s, 400 MWe, Google 200 MW offtake signed) is the more credible near-term commercial path than Helion's 2028 commitment, which would require unprecedented timeline compression and has the ITER delay record (23+ year slip against 2006 baseline) and the broader fusion forecast record (physicist survey estimates of time-to-commercial fusion have barely changed across 30 years of surveys) as the appropriate reference class rather than the Helion-specific physics milestone.

ITER's revised timeline (operation 2034, D-T operation 2035) makes it irrelevant to AI power before 2040 — it generates no electricity and is a research device.

### Advanced Nuclear NthOAK Learning

The longer-term case for advanced nuclear depends on whether US SMR deployment achieves positive learning rates analogous to Korea or France. The economics analyst's structural diagnosis, refined by the devil's advocate, holds that Korean site-level learning conditions (design freeze, integrated owner-operator-regulator, supply-chain depth, workforce continuity) are absent from the US default multi-utility multi-state deployment structure [F[EA-1]]. The alternative mechanism — factory or module-level learning rather than site-level learning — is plausible and is what modular-manufacturing advocates point to, but it is unproven as an empirical matter for the US context. The reference-class analyst's outside-view reconciliation finds that US/EU large-reactor programs since 1995 show approximately 70–85% negative-or-zero learning, with NuScale CFPP cancellation as the SMR-specific data point.

The optimistic scenario for US SMR NthOAK learning requires a single co-located fleet with a single integrated utility (the BWRX-300 Darlington model, potentially replicated at TVA Clinch River). This is achievable in principle but is not the default multi-state deployment structure that most announced SMR projects reflect. The MIT CANES assessment puts even a next US AP1000 at $6,500–7,500/kW with Vogtle lessons applied; meaningful NthOAK improvement to $5,500–6,500/kW by 2040 represents a narrow optimistic path requiring policy continuity and design convergence to 2–3 dominant designs.

### Smil Base-Rate Applied to Clean-Firm Transition

The reference-class analyst's finding on Smil's historical energy transition base rate requires a scope clarification applied by the devil's advocate [DA synthesis mandate 2]. Smil's 50–70 year figure measures global primary-energy-source transitions to 25% share of total primary energy supply or total electricity — a whole-economy metric, not a sector-specific marginal-supply-mix question. The claim that clean-firm sources will take 30–50 years to reach dominance in AI-specific power supply applies Smil as a directional constraint on rate of new-technology fleet build (regulatory cycles, supply-chain depth, workforce development) rather than as a quantitative cap on sector-specific procurement. The specific "10–20% by 2040" point estimate and associated probability from the raw finding has been withdrawn per the devil's advocate refinement; the qualitative restatement stands: clean-firm sources will represent approximately a minority share of AI power by 2040, materially below AI-urgency narrative projections, with the gap driven by the same structural gating factors that constrained historical transitions [F-RCA-F1]. The only documented break in the Smil base rate downward is solar PV (1990–2025, approximately 35 years to 8% global electricity share), which achieved that break via modularity, grid-edge deployability, and semiconductor-style learning — characteristics that clean-firm power technologies including SMR, fusion, and EGS do not share.

## 4. Multi-Axis Sustainability

### Land Use

Land use is transitioning from manageable cost to binding political constraint at leading-edge data center locations in the 2025–2030 window, but it is not a national capacity cap [F[GS-2]]. Loudoun County, Virginia (March 2025, 7-2 bipartisan) eliminated by-right data center development and now requires special exception proceedings. Prince William County, Virginia had its Digital Gateway rezoning voided in March 2026. Maricopa County, Arizona introduced the first US data-center-specific ordinance. The European precedent (Dublin, Warsaw, Madrid following London-Frankfurt-Amsterdam restrictions on a 3–5 year lag) suggests this pattern spreads. Even if Loudoun-style restrictions reach 8–10 additional counties, 3,000+ US counties have not imposed restrictions — the constraint is locally binding at prime locations but nationally non-binding.

For generation siting, the relevant binding variable is NEPA environmental review timelines (3–5 years for large utility-scale federal land projects), not BLM land quantity (19M+ acres designated open for utility solar). EU Projects of Common Interest fast-track comparison: 12–18 months for comparable projects versus 3–5 years US. The BTM solar-plus-LDES hybrid at hyperscaler campus scale requires 15,000–25,000 acres for the solar component, limiting the configuration to Sun Belt geographies.

### Materials Beyond Iridium and HALEU

Battery supply-chain concentration risk is chemistry-specific [F[GS-3]]. NMC (cobalt-bearing): extreme upstream concentration with the Democratic Republic of Congo supplying approximately 73–76% of global cobalt mine output and China holding approximately 80% of cobalt refining. LFP (cobalt-free): reduces but does not eliminate concentration risk; lithium imports are 97% from Chile and Argentina, and China holds processing dominance. Iron-air (Form Energy: iron, water, air): substantially escapes concentration risk and is the supply-chain-resilient choice, contingent on commercialization.

Solar silver is an underappreciated emerging constraint. Photovoltaic demand could account for 29–41% of projected global silver supply by 2030 under static technology assumptions; total demand may exceed supply 30–38% at high solar deployment [F[GS-3]]. However, the devil's advocate refinement [DA synthesis mandate 3] applies: the copper-metallization substitution pathway (active development in HJT and TOPCon cell architectures; TOPCon at approximately 13 mg/W silver; if copper-metallization reaches 50% market share by 2030, silver demand from solar decreases by approximately 260 million ounces annually) is a documented relief valve that the raw silver constraint finding underweights. Silver under static technology assumptions is a 5–15% cost-pressure constraint on solar deployment trajectory, not a binding supply cap on AI power buildout.

Beyond silver: rare earths for direct-drive wind (China holds approximately 85–90% of global rare-earth processing); cobalt and lithium supply concentration for lithium-ion chemistry; iron-air as the supply-chain-escape option that is still pre-commercial. Copper substitution in solar provides meaningful relief on silver; iron-air provides meaningful relief on battery concentration; wind-specific rare earth risk is unaddressed by either substitution.

### Waste and End-of-Life

Waste and end-of-life profiles differ materially by technology on a 30-year planning horizon [F[GS-4]].

Spent nuclear fuel: approximately 70,000–90,000 metric tons of heavy metal accumulated at US reactor sites, with approximately 2,000 MTHM/year generation. Yucca Mountain's NRC license is in administrative indefinite suspension; Congressional action is required. There is no interim consolidated storage facility operating. The 40-year dry-cask safety record is clean (no operational safety incidents), but regulatory complexity and cost are real 30-year liabilities for procurement planning.

PEM electrolyzer PFAS: PFSA ionomers are fluoropolymers subject to EU REACH 2.0 near-total-ban trajectory. An October 2025 study via EPA Method 1633 detected no PFAS during normal PEM operation — this is the key finding that shifts the risk assessment. Manufacturing and disposal regulatory exposure is real under EU REACH 2.0, but industry-specific electrolyzer exemptions are politically expected given EU hydrogen strategy. The risk assessment should be revised from "problematic" to "regulatory monitoring required — likely manageable via industry-specific exemptions" [F[GS-4], DA synthesis mandate in R2 already applied in GS finding].

PV recycling: EU WEEE Directive mandates 85% recovery and 80% recycling targets. US: Washington state-only mandatory take-back; no federal mandate. Approximately 24.93 million tonnes of US PV waste expected 2025–2050, representing approximately $189B in recoverable material value — sufficient private-sector incentive for recycling market development even without a federal mandate.

GPU e-waste: global 22.3% recycling rate (UN GEM 2024). AI server refresh cycles compressing from traditional 5–7 years to 18–36 months, materially accelerating end-of-life volume at industry-wide level. Industry leaders (Oracle 99.6%, Microsoft 90.9%) have circular datacenter programs; 12% of data centers have zero e-waste programs.

Iron-air end-of-life: iron oxide as primary reaction product, no hazardous materials, mature metal recycling stream — the most benign end-of-life profile among the storage technologies evaluated.

## 5. The Configurations Named in H3

### Waste-Heat Recovery and District Heating Integration

Waste-heat recovery is technically mature and operating at scale in Northern Europe. Stockholm Exergi's Open District Heating connects 30+ data centers to a 3,000 km network warming approximately 30,000 apartments annually; Microsoft-Fortum in Finland supplies approximately 40% of district heating demand for 250,000 people (live approximately 2026). EU Energy Efficiency Directive Article 23 (effective October 2025) mandates waste-heat assessment and cost-benefit analysis for all EU data centers above 1 MW [F[TIA-3]].

US adoption faces three structural barriers that are conjunctively binding on the 2030 horizon, not individually insurmountable but together making meaningful US scale-up implausible before 2035 [F[TIA-3], F[EA-4]]:

Barrier 1 — Infrastructure absence: the US has approximately 660 district heating systems (IDEA database), covering roughly 2% of the built environment versus approximately 50% in Nordic countries and approximately 13% in Germany. The Stockholm 3,000 km network took 70+ years to build.

Barrier 2 — Cooling architecture default: US data centers default to air-cooled architectures. Waste heat recovery requires warm-water cooling loops operating at 40–60°C supply temperature. Retrofitting existing air-cooled facilities costs approximately $2–8M per MW.

Barrier 3 — Regulatory and commercial framework: Virginia HB323 (2026), the first US data center waste-heat reuse bill, directs convening a working group — a pre-feasibility step, not a deployment mandate. No US equivalent of EU EED Article 23 mandate exists.

The Stockholm-style model is not replicable at meaningful US scale outside Northern Europe in the 2030 timeframe. Virginia HB323 is explicitly pre-feasibility. Industrial co-location (data center waste heat to industrial process heating, bypassing the residential district heating buildout requirement) is the realistic near-term US pathway; Minneapolis, Chicago, Boston, and NYC are the highest-probability geographies for any US district-heating integration given existing heat network infrastructure. Revenue math: at Stockholm's approximately €190,000/MW annually on 50 MW thermal output, the offset is approximately $10–20/MWh equivalent — material for a European site with EU ETS carbon value but substantially weaker in US absent carbon pricing [F[EA-4]].

### BTM Solar + LDES Hybrid at Hyperscaler Scale

The Google Minnesota project (300 MW/30 GWh Form Energy iron-air, 1,400 MW wind and 200 MW solar, approximately $1B capital commitment, 2028 installation scheduled) is the most significant single LDES deployment for AI power globally and is the empirical gate for the H9 configuration hypothesis [F[TIA-4], F[EMA-5], F[EA-5]].

A critical structural distinction: this is NOT behind-the-meter in the standard sense. The project is structured through Xcel Energy's Clean Energy Accelerator regulatory framework, with Xcel managing grid integration. Google bears the capital cost but the energy flows through the utility framework. The Clean Energy Accelerator is a Minnesota-Xcel-specific regulatory instrument that does not exist in PJM, ERCOT, or CAISO. Replication in deregulated markets requires true BTM interconnection agreements and islanding-capable architecture — substantially more complex [F[TIA-4]]. The Google Minnesota label of "BTM solar + LDES hybrid" in H9 overstates self-sufficiency; it is better described as "utility-partnered LDES at hyperscaler anchor-tenant scale."

The economics, applying the cross-agent finding on $20/kWh: at Lazard's current $35–50/kWh iron-air range plus the 45–50% round-trip efficiency penalty (requiring approximately 1.7–2× solar oversizing for equivalent net energy delivery), blended LCOE at 500 MW campus scale in the Sun Belt is approximately $90–115/MWh, not the optimistic $70–90/MWh range implied by H9 [F[EMA-5], F[EA-5]]. The H9 blended LCOE of sub-$80/MWh requires Form Energy achieving $20/kWh AND a favorable grid tariff structure. At the $30–50/kWh realistic 2030 range post-45X credits, the economics are viable on a procurement basis but not at the sub-$80/MWh level that would make the configuration clearly dominant over alternatives.

Land constraint is binding: 1.5 GW of solar for a 500 MW campus with 100-hour iron-air storage requires approximately 15,000–25,000 acres. This limits the configuration to Sun Belt geographies (Texas, Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, California desert). It is not available in Northern Virginia, Chicago suburbs, or EU urban markets. The economic constraint is real but manageable; the geographic constraint is binding.

Form Factory 1 (Weirton, WV, targeting 500 MW/year production) would consume approximately seven months of full-factory output for a single Google Minnesota installation. Fleet-scale hyperscaler deployment (10+ GW of LDES) requires Factory 2 and beyond, or competing iron-air manufacturers — neither of which exists as of 2026 [F[TIA-4]].

The 85–95% clean energy fraction is technically credible given 100-hour storage covering multi-day low-solar periods [F[EMA-5]]. The LCOE — and therefore the configuration's commercial viability — is what remains unresolved pending Georgia Power's independent cost data.

## 6. The Gas Reality Reframed: Gap-2 and Gap-3 Convergence

This is the central analytical contribution of this follow-up review. The prior synthesis treated bridge-fuel entrenchment as operating primarily through the mechanism of utility-scale CCGT filling the AI power gap via the PJM TC1 106 GW queue. The follow-up review establishes that this mechanism is physically constrained by OEM supply, and that the substitute mechanism — distributed RICE fleets and intensified existing-fleet utilization — is itself a form of fossil entrenchment that may be harder to unwind than central CCGT.

The energy-market analyst's R3 finding establishes the supply constraint via primary sources [F[EMA-6]]: GE Vernova's Q1 2026 backlog reached 100 GW with CEO stating turbines are "sold out through 2030 by end of 2026" and approximately 10 GW of manufacturing capacity remaining through 2030; Siemens Energy holds a record 58 GW backlog (year-end 2025) with S&P Global confirming US gas-fired turbine wait times "as much as seven years"; Mitsubishi Power backlog approaches 30 GW plus 20 GW in reservation agreements, with plans to double manufacturing capacity over two years. Combined three-OEM global manufacturing capacity of approximately 30–35 GW/year, expanding to approximately 40 GW/year by 2028, against combined backlogs of approximately 190 GW — representing more than five years of current production. The PJM TC1 106 GW queue is not a delivery schedule; projects without already-reserved turbine slots face 5–7 year wait times from order placement. Combined with historical PJM queue-to-commercial-operation attrition of approximately 30–50%, realistic CCGT additions to the PJM system by 2030 from TC1 projects are materially lower than the 106 GW nominal — approximately 20–40% of nominal capacity at base case.

The actual near-term fast-bridge mechanisms are: distributed RICE engines (Wärtsilä, Caterpillar, Cummins, Bergen) with 12–24 month lead times versus 5–7 years for CCGT; aeroderivative turbines with faster installation but still subject to GE Vernova's broader backlog pressure; existing CCGT fleet running at higher capacity factors (no new turbine required, lowest-friction bridge); and flexible AI training load deferral (3–21% cost reduction per arxiv:2604.05376).

The reference-class analyst's R3 finding reaches compatible conclusions via historical base-rate and project-mix decomposition [F-RCA-F6]: US CHP has plateaued at approximately 7% of national capacity since 1990; US microgrids represent 0.4% of installed capacity; approximately 95% of announced 2024–2026 hyperscaler on-site capacity is gas-fired (Crusoe Stargate Abilene 1.2–2.1 GW gas-turbine, Pacifico GW Ranch Pecos 1 GW gas, Nscale Monarch WV 2 GW RICE-gas, Edged Energy Atlanta 168 MW diesel-gas; fuel-cell microgrid is exception case below 5% of aggregate MW). The distributed pathway absorbs an estimated 20–30% of hyperscaler new-build capacity 2026–2035 (80% CI [15%, 40%]) but does not change the clean-supply outcome — it is growing the gas-firmed AI power supply, not the clean-firm AI power supply.

The convergence from independent routes [DA synthesis mandate 6, cross-agent gold standard per C5]: distributed RICE + existing fleet is the actual bridge mechanism. Distributed generation IS NOT a clean-power pathway. It is a parallel gas-entrenchment mechanism operating simultaneously with and additive to central gas entrenchment.

The stranded-asset risk is reinforced, not relieved, by this mechanism shift [DA synthesis mandate 4]: RICE engines run at 40–48% thermal efficiency versus 55–62% for combined-cycle CCGT, meaning more gas burned per MWh of AI output and higher emissions intensity per unit. A fleet of 500 × 10 MW RICE units distributed across many permitting jurisdictions is operationally harder to coordinate for retirement than 10 × 500 MW CCGT plants. Critically, BTM gas has no public utility commission pathway to retirement — PUC-regulated central CCGT can be retired through state rate-base proceedings; BTM gas has no equivalent regulatory mechanism [DA synthesis mandate 5]. Existing fleet running harder extends operational life of units that would otherwise retire, creating stranded-asset exposure in the existing fleet rather than just new builds.

The prior review's 85–95% bridge-fuel entrenchment base rate is confirmed in direction with a mechanism reframe: the vehicle shifts from purpose-built CCGT to RICE plus existing fleet extension, but the entrenchment severity is maintained or increased.

## 7. Gap-Logs

This review explicitly acknowledges findings it did not resolve:

**HALEU supply ceiling on Category B SMR aggressive-optimistic scenario**: Centrus's current HALEU production capacity of approximately 12 MT/year at Piketon versus aggregate HALEU demand of approximately 30–50 MT/year for a 4–6 GW fleet across HALEU-dependent designs was not independently quantified by any agent. The 20–30% aggressive-scenario probability for 4–6 GW Category B SMR by 2035 is conditional on HALEU supply expanding — an unresolved dependency.

**Post-2040 continental synchronous interconnect**: The long-term grid restructuring question raised implicitly by H2 (what grid structure is needed to move large amounts of clean firm power to AI load centers) was acknowledged as out of scope for this review. It is a material gap for 2040+ planning but was not addressed.

**Iron-air as LDES of record**: This review assumed Form Energy iron-air as the primary LDES technology for AI power planning, and no agent independently evaluated competing long-duration technologies (vanadium flow batteries — Invinity, Sumitomo; compressed air energy storage — Hydrostor; thermal storage — Antora). There is a hidden consensus in the agent findings that iron-air is the LDES-of-record; this assumption is untested across LDES technology alternatives. If iron-air commercialization fails or is delayed, the LDES picture changes materially and the alternatives were not evaluated.

**SMR scenarios**: The prior review's framing should disaggregate Category A (LWR restarts — higher near-term probability) from Category B (genuine new-build SMR). The 5 GW threshold for H1 is achievable only under aggressive-optimistic Category B assumptions (20–30% probability). **PEM PFAS risk**: The prior analysis should reflect the October 2025 EPA Method 1633 study finding of no PFAS detected during normal PEM operation. Risk characterization should be revised from "problematic" to "regulatory monitoring required — likely manageable via industry-specific exemptions."
