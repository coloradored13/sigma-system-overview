# Fortna: Competitive Position and the Agentic AI Opportunity

*Competitive market intelligence brief — August 2026*

## Bottom line

Fortna is a capable, mid-tier design-build integrator sitting on a genuinely useful but under-marketed deterministic asset (its owned digital-twin and slotting-simulation software), a thin and mostly partner-sourced set of AI/agentic initiatives, and a balance sheet that just changed hands. None of that is disqualifying. What it means is that Fortna's near-term opportunity in agentic technology is narrower and more specific than "catch up on AI" — and the market itself isn't ready for the sweeping version of that story anyway.

Four things matter most:

- **The ownership picture just changed.** On August 20, 2026, Fortna agreed to a debt-for-equity recapitalization that hands majority ownership to its lenders, led by Ares Management, eliminates roughly $1.8 billion of debt, and removes more than $150 million a year in interest expense. As of this writing the deal is agreed but not closed. That materially increases Fortna's investment capacity going forward, and it changes who is setting strategy and on what time horizon.
- **Fortna is not behind on AI broadly — it is behind on one specific thing, and roughly at parity with its closest peer on the rest.** Checked against a consistent, strict definition of "agentic" (systems that plan, act, and iterate with tool use, not single-shot prediction or classic optimization), most of the industry's "AI" claims — including several of Fortna's own — don't clear the bar. Manhattan Associates is the one competitor with a credible shipped lead. Against Dematic, its nearest same-business-model peer, Fortna is at par: neither has shipped agentic capability.
- **The market for agentic AI in this industry is not ready, and that's an opportunity, not just a caution.** Roughly half of fulfillment operations are still mostly manual. The binding constraint on adoption is data and process readiness, not model capability — which means selling readiness to the unready majority is a larger near-term opportunity than selling autonomy to the small instrumented minority.
- **Fortna's best move is to lead with what it can already prove, not with what it claims.** Its shipped, owned digital-twin and slotting-simulation software is a real asset that gets under-marketed relative to unproven partner-sourced AI claims. That positioning works today because most rivals are currently choosing to lead with unprovable agentic claims of their own — not because they lack the ability to demonstrate comparable tools. It's a cheap, near-term move, and it should not be sold as a durable moat.

## 1. Ownership and financial position: a recapitalization in progress

Fortna today is the product of a 2022 combination of MHS Global (a conveyor and sortation hardware manufacturer with systems-integration roots) and the pre-2022 Fortna (a design-consulting and software firm), operating under the unified FORTNA brand.[^1] That merger history is more than trivia — it's the reason Fortna can plausibly offer both hardware integration and proprietary software under one roof, a combination most competitors don't have: pure-play software vendors like Manhattan Associates and Blue Yonder don't build or integrate hardware, and OEM-led integrators like Dematic and Daifuku don't typically sell proprietary orchestration software as a standalone product.

The ownership structure has just changed. Thomas H. Lee Partners (THL) held majority ownership since a 2019 recapitalization, with a subsidiary of the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA) taking a minority stake to support the 2022 merger. On August 20, 2026, Fortna announced it had reached agreement with holders of 74% of its funded debt, plus its sponsors, to convert majority ownership to its existing lender group, led by Ares Management, in an out-of-court restructuring explicitly structured to avoid a Chapter 11 filing.[^2] The terms: roughly $1.8 billion of debt eliminated, about $150 million in new equity funded by existing lenders, and more than $150 million a year in interest expense removed. As of this writing, the transaction is **agreed but not yet closed** — Fortna's own release states it is expected to close "in coming weeks, subject to satisfaction of standard closing conditions." THL's and ADIA's residual ownership positions after closing are not disclosed anywhere in the public record and should not be assumed to be zero or unchanged.

The financial picture that led here is directionally consistent with distress, though the specific figures are thinly sourced: backlog reportedly fell from roughly $1.8 billion to roughly $1.2 billion, revenue declined in 2024 and was flat to down in 2025, and margins compressed on competitive bidding and a shift toward lower-margin parcel work.[^3] Treat those numbers as directional, not planning-grade.

Fortna's most recent publicly cited revenue figure — roughly $2.1 billion — comes from a trade-press ranking published in 2023 covering fiscal year 2022, and no fresher figure could be located.[^4] Given the deleveraging that just occurred, that figure should not be assumed to reflect the company's current trajectory. Employee headcount is similarly unreliable: two data aggregators report figures three-quarters apart (1,743 versus 3,200), with no stated methodology behind either.[^5]

**What this means practically:** the new owner group is a credit fund consortium, not a growth-equity sponsor. Credit funds that convert debt to equity typically underwrite a three-to-five-year realization window. That argues for sequencing any technology investment — agentic or otherwise — by how fast it pays back, not by long-run strategic ambition. A multi-year capability build whose payoff lands past that window is a harder sell to this ownership than it would have been to a traditional growth-equity sponsor, even though the deleveraging itself has *increased* the cash available to spend.

## 2. The competitive landscape

The market Fortna competes in is genuinely large and growing, but the precise size depends heavily on how it's scoped — published 2026 estimates for "intralogistics automation" range from roughly $31 billion to $64 billion depending on which activities are included, and no single market-research vendor's figure should be treated as ground truth.[^6] What's better corroborated across independent sources is the shape of demand: warehouse labor shortage and turnover (industry-wide turnover figures run near 36%), e-commerce volume growth, 3PL margin pressure, and a wave of reshoring/nearshoring activity are all pushing automation spending up.[^7] Order intake for the sector grew roughly 7% in 2025 with a further 6% forecast for 2026 — but that average masks extreme dispersion: Dematic and Toyota Industries Logistics Systems posted 50-65% order-intake growth through the first three quarters of 2025, TGW grew 55%, while AutoStore declined 5% and Honeywell's warehouse division fell 37% before recovering slightly in 2025.[^8] In a market growing in the single digits with that much spread, outcomes are being decided by share capture and execution, not by category tailwinds.

**The competitor set is broader than the usual shortlist.** Beyond the tier-1 names — Dematic (KION), Honeywell Intelligrated, Vanderlande (a Toyota Industries subsidiary), Daifuku (the global revenue leader at roughly $4.6 billion), SSI Schäfer, Knapp (roughly $2.1 billion), and Swisslog (KUKA) — a competitive-intelligence brief on Fortna should also include TGW Logistics (roughly $925 million), Witron, Beumer Group, and Interlake Mecalux, all of comparable scale and frequently benchmarked against Fortna in trade-press rankings.[^9] AutoStore is worth a specific correction: it is more accurately described as an integration partner than a pure rival — Fortna has published multiple joint case studies with AutoStore as a channel partner (SK Pharma, South West Healthcare, Alza), even though AutoStore also partners with several of Fortna's direct competitors.[^10] Manhattan Associates, Blue Yonder, and Infios (formerly Körber) sit one layer up the software stack (WMS rather than WES/WCS) — Fortna both competes and is frequently implemented alongside these vendors, which makes them adjacent-layer competitors rather than direct rivals.

Toyota Industries folded three separate brands — Vanderlande, Bastian Solutions, and viastore — into a single new unit, Toyota Automated Logistics, effective April 1, 2026, consolidating what had been three competing integrator brands into one.[^11] That's a structural, competitor-count-reducing event worth tracking independent of any AI narrative.

Honeywell is the sharpest cautionary case in the sector. It shipped a new modular warehouse analytics platform, Momentum Core, on April 13, 2026 — and ten days later, on April 23, announced it was selling its entire Warehouse & Workflow Solutions division (Intelligrated and Transnorm) to American Industrial Partners.[^12] A credible new software platform did not change the strategic outcome. That's direct evidence against the idea that a better software or AI layer, on its own, is sufficient to win in this market.

**The demand side isn't ready for an autonomy-led sales pitch.** Survey data from 2025 puts roughly 52% of fulfillment operations as mostly or fully manual, with only about 4% "highly automated" — and the publisher of the leading supply-chain AI adoption survey attributes low adoption specifically to "data readiness and visibility maturity," not to lack of interest.[^13] Agentic orchestration needs a live, instrumented control surface to act on; most prospects don't have one yet. That's a demand-side constraint, not a technology-maturity one, and it cuts both ways: it caps how fast an autonomy story can land, but it also means the market for "get your operation instrumented and ready" is several times larger than the market for "deploy autonomous agents today."

## 3. Where Fortna actually stands on AI — what's shipped versus what's claimed

Fortna has been active on the product side, not dormant. In roughly eighteen months it has announced five AI- or robotics-related initiatives: Autonomous Flow, an update to its FortnaWES orchestration software (shipped March 2025); a partnership with sSy.AI covering computer-vision-to-digital-twin capture and natural-language querying (announced March 2025); a machine-learning partnership with Rockwell Automation (confirmed to exist, specifics not publicly available); a partnership with Jacobi Robotics for AI-powered mixed-case palletizing (announced April 2026); and deployed sorting robotics with Geekplus.[^14]

Checked against a strict definition of agentic AI — systems that plan, act, and iterate using tools toward a goal, as distinct from classic machine-learning optimization or a chat interface — most of that list doesn't clear the bar. Autonomous Flow is described by Fortna itself as "predictive optimization algorithms" — real-time, rules-driven optimization, not multi-step autonomous reasoning. Jacobi's palletizing capability is computer-vision-driven robotic grasping and sequencing, validated in advance through digital-twin simulation — genuinely useful, but not agentic by this definition either. The one initiative that plausibly clears the bar, the sSy.AI partnership, is explicitly described in Fortna's own press release as an "R&D project" — not a shipped product.

The more revealing finding sits in what Fortna does *not* claim AI for. FortnaOptiSlot DC — its own, in-house, shipped digital-twin and slotting-simulation product — makes no AI, machine-learning, or neural-network claim anywhere on its product page. It's marketed purely as "advanced algorithms and digital twinning."[^15] That's a genuinely owned, deterministic asset, confirmed by direct inspection of the product page, and it stands in useful contrast to the partner-sourced AI initiatives: **where Fortna has proof, it doesn't claim AI; where it claims AI, it has a partnership and a self-labeled R&D project, not a proprietary shipped capability.** That asymmetry is the single most useful fact in this brief for shaping how Fortna should talk about itself (see Section 6).

Both of Fortna's disclosed AI-specific capabilities — sSy.AI and Jacobi — are partner-sourced rather than built in-house, even though Fortna maintains real in-house software and hardware engineering for its core WES/WCS/OptiSlot stack (confirmed via active engineering job postings).[^16] On the internal-operations side — engineering, proposal generation, project delivery, back office — there is no public evidence of any AI or agentic application at Fortna, in either direction. That absence should be read carefully: firms rarely have a reason to publicize internal tooling, so this is a genuine blind spot in any public-source analysis, not a confirmed gap and not a confirmed capability. Treat it as unknown, not as validated white space.

Fortna's own messaging is consistent with an advisory-first posture — company materials describe AI as enhancing human expertise rather than replacing it, with simulation and scenario-testing (not live autonomous action) as the stated value proposition for planning tools.

## 4. Checking competitors' AI claims against the same bar

Applying that same strict test evenly across the competitor set changes the picture materially from a first read of vendor marketing.

**Reclassified out — analytics or classic optimization dressed as "AI," not agentic:** Dematic's Command Center is described in Dematic's own materials as an "Analytics Platform," with "more advanced autonomous and agentic capabilities" explicitly placed on the future roadmap rather than shipped.[^17] Symbotic's routing capability is reinforcement-learning-based combinatorial pathfinding — the same category of technology as Fortna's own Autonomous Flow. GreyOrange's DeepNav is reinforcement-learning-based dispatch optimization. AutoStore's "Intelligence" platform is analytics-branded with an unconfirmed agentic architecture underneath.

**Confirmed survivors, at varying confidence:**
- **Manhattan Associates** is the strongest case in the market. It has nine named production agents live across its platform, including a specific customer deployment (a wave-coordination agent at Giant Eagle), and its natural-language requirements-to-configuration tool is described by the company as "already using the tool in targeted engagements... not a demo or a roadmap item."[^18] Even so, Manhattan's own CFO describes AI revenue contribution as "still a pretty small contribution," with roughly a tenth of its installed base having merely been "touched" by a pilot or subscription — a commercial-adoption metric, not a value-realization one. Treat Manhattan as ahead, but not as proof that agentic AI is already paying off at scale even for the strongest player in the market.
- **Locus Robotics** markets its LocusONE platform explicitly as agentic — a distinct reasoning layer running a continuous observe-decide-act loop above its dispatch engine, live at more than 150 sites — but this rests entirely on the vendor's own description, with no independent audit or named customer deployment found.
- **Blue Yonder's** story is narrower than its headlines suggest: most of its agents are advisory recommendation engines for human planners, with one specific, narrower example of autonomous action (reprioritizing orders during a disruption).

**The corrected comparison that matters most for Fortna:** against Dematic — its closest peer by business model (a design-build integrator, not a pure-play software vendor) — Fortna is roughly **at par, not behind.** Neither has shipped agentic capability that clears this bar. The earlier, broader claim that Fortna trails "a wide slice" of its competitor set doesn't survive contact with a consistently applied definition; the defensible, narrower claim is that Fortna trails Manhattan specifically, and trails Locus and part of Blue Yonder's functionality at lower confidence, since those rest on vendor self-report alone.

## 5. Why the gap exists — and why it doesn't need a single explanation

Three plausible explanations for Fortna's partner-sourced, pre-production AI posture emerged from this research, and none of them can be ruled out in favor of the others:

1. **Business-model structure.** Design-build integrators generally — not just Fortna — run on project- and equipment-pass-through revenue rather than recurring software subscriptions, which structurally funds less software-specific R&D than a subscription business like Manhattan's. Dematic is the clean test case here: it carries no comparable financial distress and still hasn't shipped agentic capability either, which suggests the pattern may be about business model, not about any one firm's balance sheet.
2. **Capital constraint.** Both of Fortna's AI partnerships were formed during 2025-2026, the same window in which the company was carrying enough debt to require the deleveraging described above. A capital-constrained R&D budget is at least as plausible an explanation as deliberate strategy.
3. **Strategic choice.** Partnering for AI and computer-vision capability rather than building in-house may simply reflect a considered make-versus-buy decision, since agentic AI isn't Fortna's core competency and integration depth is.

These aren't mutually exclusive, and this brief doesn't pick a winner. What matters practically: the capital relief from the recapitalization removes one plausible constraint going forward, but if the structural explanation dominates, that relief alone won't necessarily shift Fortna toward building in-house — the underlying economics of a pass-through-revenue business don't change just because the balance sheet does.

## 6. The product and positioning play: lead with what's provable

The strongest, cheapest move available to Fortna is to lead its market narrative with FortnaOptiSlot DC and its underlying design and simulation capability — an asset it already owns, has already shipped, and can demonstrate directly — rather than with unproven, partner-sourced AI claims.

This works today for a specific, narrow reason, and it's important to state it precisely: **it is not because competitors lack the ability to build or demonstrate comparable simulation and digital-twin tooling — several already do.** It's because, in a market where nearly every vendor is currently choosing to lead with agentic-AI claims that can't be independently verified, a vendor that demonstrates what it actually markets stands out. That's a statement about what competitors are currently choosing to emphasize, not about what they're capable of. It follows directly that this advantage is **imitable and time-limited** — any competitor could adopt the same "lead with what's provable" posture tomorrow — and it should not be marketed or internally sold as a durable moat. It's a cheap, low-risk positioning move worth making now, precisely because the underlying asset already exists and costs nothing new to promote.

Durable competitive advantage from agentic AI features specifically is unlikely — our estimate is low, on the order of one chance in six or seven. Features in this market get matched quickly (the WES/WCS software landscape is fragmented with no dominant standard), and Fortna's own AI capability is sourced from non-exclusive partnerships that competitors can replicate with the same partners or equivalent ones. The one plausible route to something more durable is if Fortna converts its own decades of accumulated system-design and site-performance data into a genuine proprietary asset — something competitors can't simply license their way into. That data asset, not the AI layer sitting on top of it, is where any real moat would have to live.

On the sales and bid side specifically, the picture splits from the product side. Agentic capability plausibly has a real effect at the margin on win rates and pipeline in the next one to two years — our estimate is in the 40-75% range for some measurable effect by 2027 — but this is best understood as a **floor, not a premium**: the risk is being penalized for having *nothing* to say about agentic AI as claims become standard across the industry, not a competitive edge from having *something* to say. The probability that agentic capability is genuinely decisive in vendor selection — as opposed to simply table stakes — is considerably lower, roughly one in four, and that narrower estimate is less battle-tested than the others in this brief and should be treated as more provisional. No evidence was found, across two independent searches, that agentic capability is currently scored as a formal criterion in intralogistics RFPs; multi-year capital-project buyers in this market weight trust and delivery track record heavily, and the industry's own worst failures illustrate why: a micro-fulfillment automation vendor filed for bankruptcy in 2024 after failing to expand pilot customers past roughly three sites each, a robotics vendor filed for insolvency in 2025 days after announcing a marquee retail partnership, and a third went from a roughly $10 SPAC listing to a $1.40 take-private in two years.[^19] These were not technology failures — they were failures to convert pilots into scale, and in at least two cases, narrative that ran ahead of delivered substance. A buyer base that has watched that pattern play out is not likely to reward unproven claims.

The practical recommendation for the bid surface: **clear the floor, then differentiate on the provable.** Carry a credible, specific agentic-AI story so silence doesn't cost Fortna a competitive penalty, but lead the actual narrative — in RFPs, in marketing, in analyst conversations — with the shipped, owned, verifiable simulation and design capability. This holds up whether the marginal-effect estimate lands near the low or high end of its range; only the framing of the recommendation changes, not the recommendation itself.

On the product roadmap: several near-term agentic-technology candidates are feasible, at different levels of autonomy — a natural-language analytics and design copilot extending the sSy.AI relationship; agentic exception-handling within WES orchestration (dynamically re-routing or re-prioritizing within a pre-approved envelope); a monitoring-diagnosis-recovery loop for the robotics and material-handling fleet; and a design/simulation assistant extending the OptiSlot direction. None of these should be pursued as a stand-alone competitive-moat bet. The most promising of them — agentic exception-handling — is really best understood as an extension of Fortna's owned integration-depth data (its experience running a multi-vendor equipment fleet across many customer sites) with agentic reasoning as a thin interface layer on top, not as an independent AI capability. That's the same pattern as the OptiSlot recommendation: differentiation should come from the owned asset underneath, with agentic AI as the interface, not the substance.

One architectural point worth flagging for any product build: agentic reasoning doesn't belong at the real-time equipment-control layer — sub-100-millisecond response budgets don't fit multi-step reasoning loops, and that layer should stay fast, deterministic, and separate. It belongs at the orchestration layer and above, where seconds-to-minutes of slack exist. But putting reasoning above the control layer solves a *timing* problem, not a *safety* one: a fast, reliable execution layer will carry out a wrong high-level decision exactly as faithfully as a right one. Any agentic capability touching an irreversible or safety-rated action needs an explicit approval or validation gate between the reasoning layer and the execution layer — not just architectural separation. This is reinforced by incoming EU product-liability rules (implementation deadline December 2026) that classify AI software as a "product" subject to strict liability for defects, a live consideration for any EU-market deployment of autonomous decision features.[^20]

## 7. The internal-operations play: the better bet, and the one nobody can see

Applying agentic AI to Fortna's own internal operations — engineering and design, sales and proposal generation, service and support, project delivery, back office — is the structurally better investment, even though it's the one surface this brief can say the least about with confidence.

It's structurally better for two reasons: it removes dependence on customer data-readiness (a real constraint on the product side, per Section 2) and on customer procurement cycles, and Fortna already owns dense, usable internal data — system designs, simulation models, historical proposals and estimates, service records, and commissioning documentation. Our estimate for the likelihood of measurable margin or productivity benefit from internal-work agentic applications by 2028 is just under even odds, modestly higher than the equivalent estimate for the product surface.

It's the surface we can say least about because it is, by nature, mostly invisible from outside the company — there's no public evidence, in either direction, of Fortna applying agentic AI to its own operations today. That's not evidence the opportunity doesn't exist; it's a structural blind spot, since firms have little reason to publicize internal tooling. Treat it as an open question, not as validated white space, and — importantly — don't market it externally even if Fortna does invest here. Claiming credit for something that can't be independently verified would repeat exactly the pattern of unproven claims this brief flags as a competitive risk elsewhere.

Priority candidates, ranked by near-term feasibility (a different question from long-run strategic value, and worth keeping separate):

1. **Sales and proposal automation.** The highest-feasibility candidate — a mature vendor category already exists, so this is a buy-and-integrate decision rather than a build, with the fastest payback of any candidate. Systems-integrator RFP responses are highly templatable (the same technical differentiators, case studies, and compliance content reused across bids), which is exactly the profile where retrieval-based AI tools outperform blank-page drafting. This is both a cost lever (lower cost per proposal) and a competitive-parity necessity — a slower or lower-quality RFP response than an AI-equipped competitor is a real share-loss risk. Because dozens of competing RFP-AI vendors already exist, this is table-stakes tooling, not a differentiator: worth doing, not worth advertising.
2. **Engineering and design AI** (CAD and design reuse, parametric design assistance). Moderate feasibility. Fortna already uses digital-twin and simulation tooling in its engineering workflow — confirmed via its own product line and a Gartner Hype Cycle mention — so the infrastructure to extend into a generative or agentic layer exists, but there's no public evidence it has been built yet. An earlier, more aggressive claim about this being Fortna's highest-value candidate rested on an industry revenue-per-employee benchmark that didn't hold up once checked against actual peer-company figures — treat the magnitude of opportunity here as genuinely unresolved rather than as the clear top priority.
3. **Service and support** (predictive maintenance triage, spare-parts recommendations, support-ticket routing). Feasibility is conditional on an unconfirmed premise: whether Fortna's installed base currently streams real-time telemetry data back to the company. No public information confirms this either way, and if it doesn't, this candidate requires building data infrastructure from scratch before an AI layer can run on it — a materially slower, less certain path than the first two candidates.
4. **Project delivery and program coordination.** Lower feasibility. Physical, multi-vendor commissioning coordination — equipment lead times, site readiness, subcontractor sequencing — is a real-world execution problem, not the document and knowledge-work problem where today's agentic tools show the most maturity.
5. **Back office** (finance, HR, general administration). Low priority — commodity horizontal tooling available to any enterprise, with no warehouse-automation-specific differentiation worth a dedicated recommendation.

Given the new ownership's likely three-to-five-year realization horizon, these candidates should be sequenced by how fast they pay back, not by long-run ambition. Tools that can be licensed and put to work immediately (sales/proposal automation, and likely engineering/design AI) fit that horizon well. Anything requiring a multi-year, ground-up data-infrastructure build first — service and support, if telemetry isn't already flowing — is a weaker near-term fit and should be gated on confirming the telemetry premise before committing capital.

One governance point applies across every internal candidate: any agent-generated output that could bind Fortna commercially or technically — an autonomous quote, a design element that reaches a customer — needs a hard human-approval gate before it goes out the door. Given the liability exposure discussed in Section 6, this is the one place where a governance-first approach is unambiguously the right call rather than a nice-to-have.

**The single highest-leverage, lowest-cost recommendation in this brief:** before deploying any agentic tool anywhere in the business, capture baseline metrics now — current win rate, cost per proposal, and engineering hours per proposal. A widely cited (and widely misread) statistic that roughly 95% of generative-AI pilots show no measurable financial return is largely an artifact of pilots that never had a documented "before" number to measure against, not of the technology failing outright.[^21] Read correctly, that statistic is an argument for measurement discipline, not a reason for pessimism — and it's a nearly free step to take. In the same spirit, start tracking bid debriefs and RFP scoring criteria now, specifically watching for when (not whether) agentic capability becomes a formally scored requirement in competitive bids. Our best estimate for that threshold is close to a coin flip on whether it arrives before 2029 — genuinely uncertain — which makes it exactly the kind of forecast better handled by direct observation than by prediction.

## 8. Risks worth planning around

- **Data-foundation failure.** The largest single risk to any agentic deployment: rolling out tools at customer sites that lack clean, real-time operational data, so each deployment requires bespoke data engineering and costs never fall with repetition. Given how much of the addressable market remains under-instrumented (Section 2), the mitigation doubles as a business opportunity: package and sell a data-readiness assessment as a standalone offering, not just a prerequisite step.
- **Wrong-surface investment.** Agentic spending lands in a product line too small to move a design-build integrator's overall P&L, while pricing and margin pressure on the core integration business goes unaddressed. Mitigation: measure any agentic investment's return as win-rate lift and bid-cost reduction on the core integration business, not as standalone software revenue — if it can't be measured that way, it isn't being managed.
- **Commoditization through partner leakage.** Capability sourced from non-exclusive partners (as both of Fortna's current AI initiatives are) gets matched quickly by competitors running the identical playbook. Mitigation: pursue exclusivity where it's affordable, or deliberately shift the differentiation to the layer partners can't copy — Fortna's own accumulated design and commissioning data.
- **Owner-horizon mismatch.** The new lender ownership group cuts a program that hasn't paid back inside its multi-year realization window. Mitigation: gate any agentic investment on near-term payback rather than long-horizon strategic value, and treat the internal-operations candidates — the fastest, most controllable payback — as the floor that survives even an impatient owner.
- **Governance incident.** An AI-generated design or quote reaches a customer without human review and produces a costly error or a loss-making contract. Mitigation: the hard approval gate described in Section 7, applied without exception to anything commercially or technically binding.

Any internal business case for agentic investment should explicitly model the probability that a pilot expands past its first two or three customer sites — that specific transition point is where the industry's worst-known failures broke down, not at the technology-proof-of-concept stage.

## 9. What this brief could not establish

Some real gaps remain, and they should shape how confidently Fortna's leadership acts on the recommendations above rather than being read past:

- **Fortna's software revenue as a share of total revenue is not public.** This is the single most consequential missing figure for judging whether agentic capability can realistically be monetized as product revenue, versus only as a bid-cycle differentiator — and it could not be found anywhere in public sources.
- **No buyer-side evidence exists that agentic capability is currently scored in competitive intralogistics RFPs.** The bid-surface recommendation in Section 6 rests on a structural mechanism (rising claims lower the cost of not making one) and one adjacent-market vendor's own earnings disclosure — not on direct evidence from inside Fortna's own market.
- **No like-for-like large-enterprise supply-chain adoption figure exists.** The roughly 10% adoption rate cited for the vertical (Section 2) comes from a survey weighted toward small and mid-market operators, smaller on average than Fortna's typical buyer — meaning true adoption within Fortna's actual addressable segment could plausibly be somewhat higher than that figure suggests.
- **Whether Fortna's AI partnerships carry exclusivity is unknown.** This materially affects how durable any resulting capability advantage would actually be, and it isn't disclosed publicly for either the sSy.AI or Jacobi Robotics relationship.
- **Deployed scale and measured customer outcomes for Autonomous Flow are not public.** Its existence and shipped status are confirmed; what it has actually delivered for customers is not.
- **THL's and ADIA's post-recapitalization ownership stakes are undisclosed**, and should not be assumed in either direction pending the transaction's close.
